How Far Can a Straight Line Take You?

Part 1

A line is a claim

Every LP model in Weeks 1–6 took its numbers as given: margins, capacities, NPVs. Somebody had to estimate those numbers. Regression is one of the main ways they get estimated: it turns past observations into a straight-line rule for predicting a new case.

A rule like that is useful, and it is also a claim. The question for this week is how far the claim holds.

Make a prediction. Cowford Properties fit a line to 20 of its leases. Leadership now wants to use that line to quote rent on a 6,000 sq ft flagship space, and the line gives $143,400 a year. How much should they trust that number?

Part 3 · Calculate

Read the line in its units

Excel or any statistics tool finds the line that keeps the dots as close to it as possible (least squares). For these 20 leases it is:

Predicted annual rent ($K) = 8.40 + 22.50 × area (thousands of sq ft)
Intercept (a)8.40
Slope (b)22.50
Leases (n)20
What does the slope, 22.50, say?

The intercept is not a rent

8.40 is the predicted rent for a lease of zero square feet: $8,400 a year for nothing. No such lease exists, and X = 0 is far below the smallest lease in the data. The intercept positions the line; it doesn't describe a real lease.

Part 4 · Interpret

How well does it fit?

R² compares two kinds of spread. First, how far rents sit from their average if you ignore area entirely. Second, how far they sit from the line once you use area. The share of the first spread that the line removes is R².

Measure the spread on the chart above

Spread around average–
Left around line–
R²–
R² = 1 − (left around line ÷ spread around average) = 1 − …

Show the spread from the average first, then from the line. Watch the red segments shrink.

So R² = 0.78: about 78% of the variation in annual rent across these 20 leases goes with differences in lease area. It is a statement about this sample. It is not an accuracy rate, and it doesn't say a forecast will be within any particular distance of the truth.

And the p-value?

The slope's p-value is below 0.001 (t = 7.99 with 18 degrees of freedom). Read it this way: if area had no linear relationship with rent, a slope this far from zero would almost never show up in a sample of 20 leases. So the association is real evidence, not noise.

It is not "a 0.1% chance the relationship is due to chance," and it says nothing about how large or useful the effect is, or whether area causes rent.

Part 5 · Evaluate

Where to stop trusting it

The line was fit on leases from 800 to 3,000 sq ft. Inside that range, the data show the straight-line pattern holds reasonably well. Outside it, the data show nothing at all, and the line just keeps going. Predicting outside the observed range is extrapolation.

Part 6 · Interpret

What the line can't tell you

Go back to the scatterplot and inspect the 1,100 sq ft lease. It rents for $48.33K, and the line predicts $33.15K: a residual of +15.18.

A leasing manager says: "That tenant is overpaying by $15K. Cut their rent at renewal or we'll lose them." What does the regression actually support?

What this line assumes

The pattern is a straight line across the range. If the residuals curved, bending up or down at the big leases, a line would mislead.

Each lease is a separate observation. Ten suites in one building, all on the same terms, would count as ten votes for what is really one decision.

Association, not cause. Bigger leases may also be in better locations or newer buildings. The slope compares leases that already exist; it doesn't say what adding space to a building would do.

One predictor only. 22% of the variation is left over. Week 12 adds location and building age to see how much of that they explain.

Part 7 · Communicate

Tell leadership

Damar Okoye, Cowford's CFO, has one rule for analysis: if the board can't follow it, it isn't finished. A good forecast message gives the number, says where it holds, and says what else to check.
