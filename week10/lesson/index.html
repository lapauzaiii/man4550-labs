Part 1

An expected date is not a promise

Your GPS says you'll arrive in 20 minutes. That's the critical path method: one number, as if nothing will go wrong. Google Maps at rush hour says 20 to 35 minutes depending on traffic. That's PERT. Same trip, a more honest picture of it.

Big projects show why the honest picture matters. Brightline began planning its Miami–Orlando rail line in 2012 and started construction in 2015. Trains to Orlando began running in September 2023. Each piece was planned with a duration; the uncertainty in those durations added up to years.

Make a prediction. The warehouse's expected duration is 18.67 weeks. Leadership wants to promise the client completion in 19 weeks. How likely is Cowford to keep that promise?

Part 2

Where the uncertainty lives

PERT asks for three estimates of each activity: optimistic (a), most likely (m), and pessimistic (b). From them you get an expected time and a variance. The variance measures how wide the range is: how unsure we are about that activity.

Expected time = (a + 4m + b) ÷ 6   ·   Variance = ((b − a) ÷ 6)²

Part 3 · Calculate

Add variances, then take the square root

The finish date depends on the critical path, so the project's uncertainty comes from the activities on it. Add their variances to get the project variance. Then take the square root to get the project's standard deviation, which is back in weeks.

Critical-path variances

Project variance0.111 + 0.111 + 0.444 + 0.694 + 0.111 + 0.028

Two mistakes that inflate the risk

Adding standard deviations. The six critical activities' standard deviations add to 2.67 weeks, more than double the correct 1.22. Variances of independent activities add; standard deviations don't.

Including every activity. Adding D, E, and F's variances too gives 2.11 and a standard deviation of 1.45 weeks. Those activities have float, so they don't set the finish date.

Part 4 · Calculate

Turn a deadline into a probability

PERT treats the finish date as roughly normal, centered on 18.67 weeks with a standard deviation of 1.22. The Z-score says how many standard deviations the deadline sits past the expected finish. The normal table (or Excel) turns Z into the probability of finishing by the deadline.

Z = (deadline − 18.67) ÷ 1.22   ·   Excel: =NORM.S.DIST(Z, TRUE)

The client's contract requires completion by November 15, which is 20 weeks after the project starts. That gives Z = 1.09 and about an 86% chance of finishing on time. Now set the slider to 19 weeks and check your prediction from Part 1.

Part 5 · Decide

Choose a date you can stand behind

Part 4 started with a date and found the probability. Managers often need the reverse: start with how confident they need to be, and find the date. Look up the z for that confidence level and add that many standard deviations to the expected finish.

Commitment date = 18.67 + z × 1.22   ·   80%: z = 0.84 · 90%: z = 1.28 · 95%: z = 1.645

A later promise buys reliability, not speed

Nothing about the project changed between those two cards. Same activities, same expected finish. The only thing that moved is the promise. A promise set at the expected duration is kept only half the time, which is why commitments are set at a higher percentile.

Part 6 · Interpret

What actually changes the odds

Moving the deadline changes the promise. To change the project itself, a manager has two levers on the critical path: make it shorter, or make it more predictable. Activity G, the HVAC install, is the obvious place to start. It is on the critical path and carries the largest variance, 0.69 of the 1.50 total.

Try both levers against the November 15 deadline

Expected finish18.67 wk
Std. deviation1.22 wk
P(by Nov 15)86.2%

Start with the baseline: an 86% chance of finishing by November 15.

What this calculation assumes

That activity times are independent, so one delay doesn't cause another. That the total is roughly normal. And that the critical path stays the critical path. When a near-critical path like D's runs long, it can take over, and the real risk is higher than PERT says. The Week 10 lab shows this by simulating the project thousands of times.

Part 7 · Communicate

Tell the board

The schedule isn't only Logistics' problem. Cowford Professional Services has a three-year client contract, $340,000 a year, that is voided if the warehouse misses November 15. So an 86% chance of finishing on time is also a 14% chance that another division loses a client.

The Professional Services division head asks: "Will the warehouse be ready by November 15?" Which answer would you give?

A different question, for Week 13

Whether the expansion is worth its $950,000 cost is a separate decision. Answering it properly needs the contract's profit margin rather than its revenue, the timing of the cash flows, and the other options on the table. Week 13's decision analysis takes it up, with this week's probability as one of the inputs.
