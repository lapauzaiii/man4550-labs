Part 1

One decision, three options

Cowford Holdings needs more warehouse space in Jacksonville. The board can do one of three things: build the full expansion ($950K), build a phased expansion ($520K now, more later if demand shows up), or defer and lease overflow space. Nobody knows whether demand will be high, moderate, or low over the next three years.

Every business decision you'll face looks like this: a few options you control, a future you don't, and money riding on how they combine.

Gut check before any math. Which would you recommend to the board?

Part 2

Read the payoff table

A payoff table lays the decision out in one grid. Rows are the alternatives you choose among. Columns are the states of nature: possible futures you don't control. Each cell is what you'd end up with if you chose that row and that column happened. Here the cells are three-year net results in thousands of dollars ($K), assuming the project finishes on time.

Lola saysA minus sign is a loss. If the board builds the full expansion and demand comes in low, Cowford loses $610K. Find that cell before you go on.
Risk or uncertainty?

If you can attach believable probabilities to the columns, you're deciding under risk, and you can use expected value (Part 4). If you can't, you're deciding under uncertainty, and you fall back on rules like the ones in Part 3. Here, Cowford's demand forecast supplies the probabilities in the bottom row.

Part 3 · Decide without probabilities

The optimist and the pessimist

Suppose nobody trusted the forecast. Two simple rules still give an answer, and each one reflects a personality.

The optimist · maximax

Look at each row's best payoff. Pick the row whose best is highest.

The pessimist · maximin

Look at each row's worst payoff. Pick the row whose worst is least bad.

Which alternative does the optimist (maximax) pick?

Which alternative does the pessimist (maximin) pick?

Notice the problem: the two rules give opposite answers, and each one ignores half the table. The optimist never looks at the losses. The pessimist never looks at the upside. We need a rule that uses everything.

Part 4 · Calculate

The realist: expected value

Expected monetary value (EMV) weights each payoff by its probability and adds them up. It's the average result you'd get if you could make this same decision many times.

EMV = (P(high) × high payoff) + (P(moderate) × moderate payoff) + (P(low) × low payoff)

All three EMVs (move the forecast to see what changes)

P(moderate) = whatever is left: 45%

Full354.5
Phased361.5
Defer178.5

EMV is an input, not the decision

At the forecast, full and phased are only $7K apart on EMV. But full expansion's worst case is a $610K loss, and phased's is $180K. That's a $430K difference in how badly things could go. A firm that can't absorb a $610K hit shouldn't let a $7K edge make the call. EMV tells you the average; the worst case tells you whether you can survive the bad draw.

Part 5 · Calculate

What is a crystal ball worth?

Imagine a consultant who could tell the board, for certain, which demand state will happen. With that crystal ball, the board would pick the best plan for each state: full expansion if demand is high (1,180), phased if moderate (410), defer if low (60). Weight those by the probabilities and you get the expected value with perfect information.

EVwPI = 0.30 × 1,180 + 0.45 × 410 + 0.25 × 60 = 354 + 184.5 + 15 = 553.5  ·  EVPI = EVwPI − best EMV

EVPI is a ceiling

It's the most perfect information could be worth. Real market studies are never perfect, so they're worth less than EVPI. If someone quotes the board $250K for a study, the answer is no before you even read the proposal.

Part 6 · Interpret

Add the schedule risk

The payoff table assumed on-time completion. But you know from Week 10 there's only an 86.2% chance the full expansion finishes by November 15. If it's late, the Professional Services contract is voided, a $1,020K loss (three years at $340K, as the board has agreed to count it). Phased and defer don't carry that risk.

A decision tree shows this. Squares are decisions you make; circles are chance events. You solve it from right to left: at each circle, take the probability-weighted average.

Full expansion, with schedule risk = 354.5 − (1 − p) × 1,020
Full, risk-adjusted213.7
Phased361.5
Defer178.5

Lola saysThat 86.2% is the same number you calculated in Week 10. The course has been building to this: one week's answer becomes the next week's input. Week 14 does it again with the whole $2.4M budget.
Go further (optional): minimax regret and breakeven probabilities

Regret is how much you'd kick yourself: for each state, the best payoff available minus what you got. Full expansion's regrets are 0 (high), 70 (moderate), and 670 (low). Phased: 440, 0, 240. Defer: 920, 220, 0. Minimax regret picks the plan whose largest regret is smallest: phased (440).

Breakeven: how reliable would the schedule need to be for full expansion to match defer? Set 354.5 − (1 − p) × 1,020 = 178.5 and you get p ≈ 0.83. To match phased you'd need p above 1.0, which is impossible: at this forecast, full expansion can't catch phased however good the schedule gets.
